# Limitations

## What the checks can and cannot do

- The scripts match fixed patterns. They report places to review; they do not decide whether meaning was kept or whether the text is easy to read. A run with no findings does not prove anything about meaning.
- Lint counts are not a score. A rise or fall in "Review findings" says nothing about readability.
- The record from `readyasu_check.py` shows that the checks ran on a final draft with a given hash. Anyone who can edit both the draft and the record can change them; the record helps find skipped runs, it does not prevent tampering or prove human approval.

## Known gaps in lint

- Word lists match surface forms. A listed word used in its literal or technical sense ("unlock the door", "robust statistics", "elevate the platform by 2 meters") is still reported; use `.readyasu-words/allow.txt` or keep the word and give the reason.
- Word lists, project words and the pattern rules see words inside underscore emphasis (`_seamless_`, `__leverage__`), but not a word inside a snake_case name that is not in backticks (`enable_seamless_mode`).
- Words mentioned as examples inside quotation marks (a style guide that says to avoid "delve") are reported like any other use. Only interjections such as "Sure!" are treated as dialogue inside double quotation marks (or with `--doc-type` essay, script or mail); dialogue in single quotation marks is reported.
- Rhythm-level AI patterns (every paragraph the same length, a "rule of three" in every sentence, a summary sentence at the end of every paragraph) are not detected.
- Negative contrast, personification and self-answered questions are detected only in common forms. "Not X, but Y" is reported only with an article or "about" after "not" (in a heading also "just", "only", "merely" or "simply", so "## Not supported, but planned" is not reported) and, after "but", a noun phrase that starts with an article, a possessive, "about" or "rather", or a single word that ends the clause ("not a bug, but intended behavior" is missed). That noun phrase must end the clause within about four words or go on with a preposition, "that", "which" or a pronoun ("but a platform for building products"); a verb after it makes the sentence a concession, which is not reported ("not a blocker, but the team should review it", "but the default timeout went from 30s to 10s", "but the tests pass", "but the patch works"). The verb test is a guess from word shapes (modals, a short list of irregular past forms, -ed words, a word after a plural, an -s word that ends the clause after a word that does not look like an adjective or a determiner), so a real contrast with such a word is missed ("but a feature requested by users", "but the sales team", "but the user needs."), and a concession whose noun looks like an adjective is reported ("but the initiative helps."). A negation in a clause that opens with if, when, although, because, since, while, once, until, as and similar words is not reported, so a real contrast inside such a clause is missed too. A subject in lower case is checked only as a pronoun right after a colon when a noun phrase, "just" or a verb is denied ("The truth is simple: it isn't a tool. It's a mindset."; "Here's the thing: it's not hard. It's barely even cooking." is missed), or as a noun of one or two words after a reminder that opens the sentence ("Remember, onboarding isn't a sprint; it's a marathon."; the same after "To be clear," or "But" is missed). A capitalized word at the start of a line counts as the start of a sentence, after emoji too ("**The big shift**" then "This isn't a tool. It's a mindset."), so in hard-wrapped text a line that starts mid-sentence with a capitalized subject is checked as a sentence. In the one-sentence form, a capitalized word before the negation, or one to three words that start the sentence, count as the subject ("React isn't a framework; it's a library.", "Remote work isn't a perk; it's a paradigm shift."), so any short opening is checked the same way ("Honestly the fix isn't slow, it's ..."), while a longer subject without a determiner is missed ("Working from home every day isn't a perk; it's ..."); the two subjects must agree (he and he, a noun phrase and it or they). Neither form is reported for "it's" and a past participle, so a passive with "it's" is missed ("This isn't a bug. It's designed to work this way."), or for a dummy "it" before one of a short list of adjectives (clear, possible, likely, easy, known, necessary and similar) and a to-, that- or wh-clause; "It isn't enough to ship features. It's ..." is still reported. A sentence that opens with please, just, never, kindly, always, simply or do is an imperative, not a subject. The two-sentence form is reported only when both sentences have the same subject (a pronoun, or one to three words that start the sentence, followed by "it" or "they"; so a longer subject, "The new billing service isn't slow. It's ...", is missed, and any short opening before the negation counts, "In practice it isn't X. It's Y.") and verb (including common irregular and doubled past forms: "build" and "built", "stop" and "stopped"; "just", "simply", "merely", "only" or "really" may come before the denied verb). Neither the two-sentence form nor the one-sentence form ("It isn't X; it's Y") is reported when either part states a status or a place (sure, ready, done, available, affected, active, enabled, expired, open, fixed, deployed and similar; in, on, at, off), goes on with an -ing form, or the second part goes on with "still", "not" or "also" ("It's not a bug. It's working as designed.", "It isn't in the cloud, it's on your laptop." and "The API isn't stable yet; it's still in beta." are not reported); in the one-sentence form, "just", "only", "merely" or "simply" after the negation keeps it reported. The two-sentence form is not reported for a change over time: the first sentence ends with "at first", "back then", "at the time", "until recently" or "... ago", or the second ends within a few words with now, today, anymore, these days or again ("I didn't like it at first. I like it now."), so a real contrast that ends that way is missed ("We don't sell software. We sell outcomes now."). Personification is checked only for software and document subjects (the tool, the system, the code, the logic); "the agent" and "the assistant" are left out because they are often people, and "server" and "class" count only after a software word ("the web server", "the base class"), so "The server knows which client sent the request" is missed. Of the negated forms only "doesn't care" is reported ("The scheduler doesn't care that rows went missing"); "doesn't know", "doesn't remember" and "doesn't understand" usually state what a program has no data for, so their personifying uses are missed.
- The looser "In today's ... landscape" opener is reported only when the noun ends the phrase (a comma, a dash or the end of the sentence follows) and only modifiers come before it, so "In today's fast-paced business environment teams must adapt" is missed (the fixed phrases in stock-phrases.txt, such as "In today's fast-paced world", are still found).
- Interjections ("Sure!", "Great question") are reported only at the start of a block, a table cell, a sentence or a line that starts with a capital, so one that follows a comma is missed.
- An assistant's preamble without an interjection ("Here's a polished version of your email:", "Here's a reply you can send to Jordan:") is reported only in the first paragraph; a revision lead-in needs a colon after it, and an offer of a drafted mail needs a greeting, quotation, code block or `Subject:` line after it (a revision lead-in in mail needs one too); a mail noun without a verb ("Here's a draft email:") and a revision of something that is not a text ("Here's the updated invitation:") need a greeting or `Subject:` line. A thematic break between the preamble and the mail is skipped. A mail noun without a verb needs a sign that it is offered as a draft ("a", "an", "your", possible, suggested, proposed, draft, revised), so "Here's the email:" before a drafted mail is missed, and a noun followed by "from ..." or by a clause with its own subject and a past-tense verb is never reported, so an assistant's "Here's a draft I wrote based on your notes:" is missed too (one with "for you" is still reported). After an interjection, a lead-in whose paragraph goes on with another sentence is reported only when a greeting or `Subject:` line follows the paragraph or the paragraph ends with a colon, and without an interjection such a lead-in is missed ("Here's a draft email you can send to your landlord. I kept it polite but firm." then "Dear Mr. Lee,"). A lead-in that starts with "Below is" or "Below are", or "I've revised (rewritten, polished, edited ...) your <text>", is reported without an interjection only when a greeting or `Subject:` line follows its colon, so one before a drafted post or bio ("Below is a polished version of your LinkedIn post:") is missed. So a preamble that ends with a period before the revised text, one later in the document, and one that names a genre the pattern does not know ("Here's a LinkedIn post celebrating your promotion:", "Here are the release notes for version 3.2.0:", even before `---`) are missed, and a writer's own first paragraph such as "Here is the revised version of the config:" or "Here's an updated version of your function:" in a PR or a forum answer is reported.
- In mail, an offer about the drafted mail ("Let me know if you'd like me to adjust the tone") is reported only after the mail's sign-off (a closing such as "Best," with a comma or a name line after it) or in the paragraph right after a thematic break, and only when the sentence names the tone, the wording, the formality, a shorter or another version, "adjust it" or "make it more ..."; an offer at the end of the mail's last paragraph is missed, and a writer's postscript about the tone after the sign-off is reported. A greeting or a header line (`From:` or `Sent:` at the start of a paragraph line, `On ... wrote:`, `-----Original Message-----`, `---------- Forwarded message ----------`) starts a new message, so a writer's offer after an earlier message in a pasted thread is not reported, also when the quoted text follows the header in the same paragraph; a message in a thread with neither a greeting nor such a header (only a name and a date above it) still counts the earlier sign-off. Outside mail, after a thematic break with no greeting or sign-off before it, an offer needs words that name a draft ("this draft", "adjust the tone", "a shorter version of the email", "edit the message") or the assistant offering to change the text itself ("want me to", "would you like me to", "I can also"), unless the paragraph already has assistant chat; so "Feel free to personalize it with your own projects!" alone after a drafted post is missed, and a template's closing "Feel free to edit the message to fit your incident." is reported.
- In slides, notes to the requester are recognized only before the first heading, `---` or `Slide 1` line and after the last `---` when no heading follows it. A closing offer after the last slide of a deck without `---` separators is still reported, and the chat residue in a first slide that has no heading and comes before the first `---` (or in a last slide without a heading after the last `---`) is not reported.
- bold_label_list treats a run of bold labels that are all speaker names (one to three capitalized words or initials, with an optional number and a note of up to 40 characters in parentheses: "Speaker 1", "P2", "DEV (into the phone, whispering)") and repeat as the turns of a dialogue, so a repeated numbered label ("**Step 1:**", "**Step 2:**", "**Step 1:**") is missed too, so a repeated summary label in capitals ("**Pros:**", "**Cons:**", "**Pros:**") is missed; in script, any run of such labels is exempt, so section labels in a video script ("**Hook:**", "**Body:**") are missed there. heavy_bold does not count a bold label at the start of any line, so bold labels used as emphasis at line starts are missed. sentence_start_repeat ends a run where the speaker of such a dialogue changes, so a repeated opener across turns is missed, and in script a run across speaker names that do not repeat is exempt.
- Emoji are found from a fixed table (Unicode 15). A symbol that is an emoji only with the emoji selector U+FE0F (ℹ️, ▶️, ‼️, ➡️, ©️) is reported only with it. The other emoji among the miscellaneous symbols and dingbats (☀, ✈, ❤, ⚠, ✔) are reported even without it, because many systems draw them as emoji, also where they are meant as plain symbols (♠ ♥ in a card game). Arrows and symbols that are not emoji (→, ➜, ♪, ★, ✓) and a symbol followed by the text selector U+FE0E are not reported; emoji added after Unicode 15 outside U+1F000–U+1FAFF are missed.
- Sentence splitting uses punctuation and a short list of abbreviations; unusual abbreviations can split a sentence in two. Words that are also abbreviations before a number (no, max, fig, Dec) end a sentence unless a digit follows, and a single capital letter is treated as an initial only before a capitalized word that does not usually start a sentence ("John F. Kennedy", but "Plan B. It was cheaper."), so "Plan B. Kennedy approved it." stays one sentence. An abbreviation right after a number ("450 ms.", "5 p.m.") ends a sentence only before a capitalized word that usually starts one, other than "I" ("Meet at 5 p.m. The room is booked." is two sentences, "at 5 p.m. Smith joined" and "At 9 a.m. I restarted the service." one, so "We left at 5 p.m. I stayed." is one sentence too). Emoji right after the end mark stay with the sentence when a new sentence or the end follows ("We shipped v2! 🎉" then "Thanks to everyone."), so the exclamation mark is still found. Inline code or a URL after sentence-ending punctuation counts as a capitalized word and starts a new sentence ("The fix? `retry()` now wraps every call." is two sentences), also after an unlisted abbreviation.
- The em_dash rule skips timeline entries, fields, ranges and attributions by pattern: a time, date, version or numbered label at the start of any line or item, alone or as a range ("09:12", "Dec 5", "2025-12-03", "2025/12/03", "03.12.2025", "2026-11-13 (Thu)", "Day 2", "Step 1", "Part II", "Option A", "Slide 1", "Section 3", "Act II", "10:00–10:15", "9–10 am", "Dec 3–5", "Version 2.1", "1.2.0", "v1.0–v1.4", "2.0.0 – 2.3.1", "Last updated: March 2026", a weekday with a date in parentheses, "Monday (Mar 3)", "Tue (11/12)"; also on a hard-wrapped line, so "We met at" then "10:00 — it was short." is missed), a spaced dash between two range endpoints (numbers with a currency sign, %, am/pm or a common unit such as kg, GB, ms, days or cups, versions, Q1–Q4, capitalized month and weekday names as whole words, so "3 bugs — Janet" is checked), inline code or a URL (also in bold or as link text) that is all that comes before the dash on its line, alone or as aliases joined by a comma, a slash or "or" (an option list: "`-h`, `--help` — show help"), a dash that starts a line in plain text after a line that ends with a colon or next to a line that starts with the same dash (bullets in plain-text minutes or mail), a capitalized label of up to three words with a colon after the dash ("— Owner: Marco"), and a line that is only a dash and a capitalized name after a sentence ("— Jeff Atwood", "— Plato, Republic"). On a line with no sentence end after the dash, it also skips a dash after any capitalized word and a number at the start of the line ("## Milestone 2 — Beta"; so "Apollo 11 — the mission" is missed, while "Python 3.9 – the oldest we support – is end of life." is reported) and after an all-capitals label of up to four words (a stage direction, "**[COLD OPEN — ambient sound]**"; so "NOTE — this one is fine" is missed, while "[UPDATE — the fix is now live.]" is reported), and on a screenplay scene heading that starts with "INT.", "EXT.", "INT./EXT." or "I/E." ("INT. KITCHEN — NIGHT"; a heading without the period in mixed case, "INT Kitchen — Night", is reported). It also skips a dash after inline code followed by placeholders in capitals or angle brackets ("`--level` LEVEL — show only ..."), a dash after only names on the paragraph under a man page's `NAME` heading, and a dash before a date, a due time (next meeting, this week, by Friday, due Fri 20 Nov, ETA: Friday, by 5 pm on Friday, before the HR meeting, EOD) or, in a list item, an owner (one to a few capitalized names or @handles) that ends a list item or a title line (a heading, or the first line of a paragraph with no sentence end before the dash, no period after and no lower-case continuation on the next line). So a clause dash between two such tokens ("We grew 20% – 30 new customers signed"), before such a tag ("- We rebuilt the parser — Huge Win", "Our launch slipped — next week"), or after inline code and a capitalized acronym ("`retry()` API — ...") is missed, and other separators are still reported (a new mail's `Subject:` line, "On-call handoff — notes"); a reply or forward `Subject:` line (`Re:`, `Fwd:`) or one in a pasted message's header is skipped. A dash after a news dateline that opens a paragraph is skipped when a capitalized word follows it (a place in capitals, optionally with a state, a date and an agency: "HARBOR CITY — Andrés ...", "SAN FRANCISCO, March 3, 2025 — Acme ...", "WASHINGTON (AP) — ..."), so a clause dash after other words in capitals that open a paragraph is missed unless one of them is a common label word ("GREEN LIGHT — The board approved the budget." is missed; "UPDATE — The fix is live." and "BIG NEWS — We launched." are reported).
- fragment_run does not count a sentence that starts with a common instruction verb and has a word after it ("Open Settings.", "Click Save."), so a run of short slogans that start with such a verb ("Sign up now. Start free.") is missed.
- hedge_stack skips a modal and adverb after a question word that opens the clause or a negation or "as ... as" among the four words before it ("What could possibly go wrong?"); a real stacked hedge in such a clause is missed.
- Markdown parsing covers headings (ATX and setext), lists (a line indented at least to an item's text belongs to the item, even after a blank line or a fence; as in CommonMark, a new list interrupts a paragraph only with a bullet or "1.", so a wrapped line that starts with "1998." continues the paragraph, also inside a list item, while a sibling item ("2." after "1.") still starts an item; inside an item, a fence indented four or more columns past the item's text after a blank line is indented code, and a fence may open on the marker line, `1. ```bash`), block quotes (with lazy continuation lines and fenced code inside), tables, fenced and indented code (indented code also right after a heading, a rule, a table, or the line that ends a `<pre>` block or an HTML comment, but not right after a paragraph line, which it continues, or inside an HTML block such as `<div>` or `<p>`, which runs to a blank line; an indented line without `|` right after a table row is code, not a row), HTML comments, `<pre>`, `<script>`, `<style>` and `<textarea>` blocks (their content, up to the line with the closing tag, is treated as code; a block that is never closed runs to the end of the document, as in CommonMark; inside a list item the opening tag may be indented up to three columns past the item's text) and YAML or TOML front matter (only with a key or comment on line 2 and a closing line). A block quote indented four or more spaces inside a list item is checked as text. Deeply nested structures and raw HTML blocks may be classified roughly: a line that starts with a block-level tag (`<div>`, `<p>`, `<details>`) or holds a single tag is skipped, but text on the lines between `<div>` and `</div>` is checked, indented or not.

## Known gaps in diff

- Sentences are paired by similarity. A deleted sentence and an added sentence that are similar enough are compared as a moved sentence, and half of a split sentence is joined to its neighbor. When a rewrite reorders or merges many sentences, a change may still be reported against a neighboring sentence, or a number may be listed as removed in one place and added in another.
- Negation, hedges, permission, obligation, conditions and scope are compared by counting marker words in paired sentences. Changes expressed without those words (an antonym such as "succeeded" → "failed", a verb that changes direction, "increase" → "decrease") are not caught. Read every changed sentence.
- "Names in a different order", names moved between sentences or lines, and names gone or replaced use capitalized words. A word at the start of a sentence counts as a name for these checks only when it is also capitalized in the middle of some sentence (for added actors: when it is followed by a verb and does not appear in lower case anywhere). A capitalized word that appears in lower case in the final draft ("Peak latency" folded to "peak latency") is not treated as a gone name. Swapped roles described with common nouns ("the client asked the vendor") are not caught.
- Added evaluations and recommendations ("a big improvement", "which is excellent", "we recommend") are not detected; read for them.
- "over" and "under" before a number are read as approximations, also when they are prepositions ("spread over 3 regions").
- Headings are not used for the "name" check, because they are often in Title Case.
- Units are compared only when written right after the number (one qualifier word may sit between: "30 business days"). Months and weekdays are compared by name; dates written only as numbers are compared as numbers.
- "One" is treated as a pronoun, not the number 1, only in "no one", "one another", and after "the", "this", "old", "new" and similar words at the end of a phrase or before "who", "that" or "which" ("the old one."). A count written that way ("keep the one.") is not compared.
- Added actors are found from a list of role words and capitalized names.
- Fact gaps are a partial list of references (a deadline, a vague owner or time), figures, effects, degree words and changes without values.
- A decimal's trailing zero is ignored ("0.50" equals 0.5, "1.0" equals 1), except after a product or version word ("Python 3.10", "Node 18.20", "version 2.10"); a version written without such a word ("3.10 is out") is compared as 3.1.
- "unable to" counts as a negation ("could not"); "failed to" does not, so "failed to deliver on time" → "delivered late" passes, and so does "failed to deliver" → "delivered".
- Role and term acronyms (CEO, ARR, QA...) are compared only when one replaces another in the same sentence; one that disappears is listed only under the words that disappeared.

## What has been tested

- The scripts are tested with pytest (unit tests for the text handling, every lint rule, every meaning-risk kind, check run and verify, and the consistency of the documents with the scripts) on Linux with Python 3.13. CI runs the tests on Python 3.9 and 3.14 on Linux and 3.12 on Windows.
- The rewriting procedure (SKILL.md) has not yet been measured by running a model on a test set, and installs through the plugin, skills CLI and GitHub CLI have not been tried.
